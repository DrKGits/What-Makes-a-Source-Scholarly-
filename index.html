What Makes a Source "Scholarly"?

Peer-reviewed vs. everything else

Not everything with an author and a reference list counts. A scholarly source is written by experts, for experts, and reviewed by other experts before it ever reaches you. That last part (the reviewing) is what "scholarly source" is really asking for. Here is how to tell the real thing from a convincing lookalike.

On this page

  1. What a scholarly source is (and is not)
  2. Peer-reviewed vs. not peer-reviewed
  3. Why a textbook is not always scholarly

1What a scholarly source is (and is not)

The real thing vs. a convincing lookalike

✓ What it has LOOK FOR THIS
  • Written by named experts with credentials in the field
  • Published in an academic journal or by a university or academic press
  • Includes original research, a methods section or theoretical argument, and a full reference list
  • Aimed at scholars and students, not the general public
The tell: you can trace every claim to evidence, and other researchers are expected to build on or challenge it.
✗ What it is not STAY CAUTIOUS
  • Magazine and news articles
  • Blog posts
  • Most websites (even GCU's)
  • Marketing content and opinion pieces

These can be well-written and even accurate, but they have not been vetted by a panel of experts, and their goal is often to inform quickly or persuade, not to withstand scrutiny.

The tell: no methods, no citations you can chase down, and a writer whose job is readership, not rigor.

2Peer-reviewed vs. not peer-reviewed

Quality control by the author's toughest audience

1

What "peer review" means

Before publication, other experts read the work anonymously and can demand fixes, or reject it outright. It is quality control by the author's toughest audience: their peers.

2

Peer-reviewed = strongest

A study in a peer-reviewed journal has cleared that gauntlet. This is the gold standard your assignments want. Look for "refereed" or "peer-reviewed" in the database filters.

3

Scholarly ≠ always reviewed

Some scholarly work (conference papers, dissertations, some book chapters) is credible but not formally peer-reviewed. Useful, but check what your rubric requires.

3Why a textbook is not always scholarly

Tertiary sources summarize; primary sources prove

Textbooks feel authoritative: they are thick, cited, and written by professors. But a textbook summarizes findings; it rarely presents original research, and most are not peer-reviewed the way journal articles are. They are tertiary sources: great for background and orientation, weak as the scholarly evidence a graduate paper is supposed to lean on.

Use them to learn the terrain, then cite the primary studies they point you toward.

✗ The trap

Citing your textbook as proof of a claim: "Northouse (2022) says transformational leadership works."

That is a summary of other people's research, one step removed from the evidence.
✓ Better

Follow the textbook to its sources. Find the original Bass or Avolio study it summarized and cite that.

Now you are citing the research itself, which is exactly what the rubric rewards.
The test: Expert-written→ Peer-reviewed→ Traceable evidence
